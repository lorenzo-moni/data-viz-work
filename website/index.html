§ 03 — Our Reading

Fifteen findings. One thesis: most games die fast, but the ones that survive share a handful of measurable traits. Every chart below is computed live from the dataset.

§ 01

The Graveyard

Most games are already dead.

We tracked every game with enough signal — a Steam page, a player history, a community engagement record. Then we asked: how many of those players are still here?

The median game retains fewer than 4% of its engaged players. The rest stopped showing up.

§ 02

The Flood

2016 was the year the industry drowned itself.

More games were released that year than any other in our dataset. Most of them are now silent — their communities gone, their servers empty.

The color of each bar is the median vitality of that year's cohort today. Watch the bars go from orange to red as you move right toward 2020.

§ 03

Most Games Are Never Finished

The median completion rate is 9.1%.

Across every game in the dataset, only one in ten engaged players ever reaches the credits. The rest give up, get bored, or move on.

Some genres are designed for this: puzzle games get played to completion, MMOs never are. The gap matters for what comes next.

Left: completion_rate distribution. Right: median completion by primary genre.

§ 04

The Completion Paradox

The games people finish are not the ones that survive.

Completion rate and alive_ratio have a negative correlation of −0.175. The games players never finish — MMOs, strategy titles, simulations — are the ones still drawing crowds years later.

Games designed to end, die. Games designed to continue, live.

§ 05

The Decay Curve

Vitality erodes with age — but the upper tail refuses to die.

The median game loses most of its active players within three years. But the 90th percentile holds steady for decades. The curve splits into two populations: the typical death and the exceptional survival.

Overlay a genre to see which ones beat the median curve and which ones collapse faster than the average.

Ribbon = p25–p90 band. Solid line = median. Click genre chips to overlay.

§ 06

Genre Lifecycles

Every genre has its own death curve.

Strategy and Massively Multiplayer games age like wine — their vitality barely bends. Action and Adventure games age like milk — sharp drop-off by year three.

The genre you ship in is a stronger predictor of longevity than your launch size or your review score.

§ 07

The DNA of Immortality

Immortal games cluster in a few genres.

We compare two cohorts of pre-2018 games: immortals (alive_ratio > 0.10) and mortals (alive_ratio ≤ 0.03). The genre composition is starkly different.

RPG is overrepresented in immortals by more than 13 percentage points. Action and Adventure lean mortal.

§ 08

Critics vs Community

A high Metacritic score does not keep players coming back.

We plot both critic score and community rating against alive_ratio. The Pearson correlations tell the story: Metacritic barely moves the needle, community rating does slightly better — but neither is strong.

Scores predict quality. They don't predict survival.

Click any quadrant to see the games that fall in it — e.g. high score + low survival.

§ 09

The Price of Survival

Free-to-play titles dominate longevity rankings.

When we group games by launch price and compare their current vitality, free-to-play games sit clearly at the top. The barrier to entry shapes the size and loyalty of the community that forms around a game.

Price is a retention lever, not just a revenue decision.

Median alive_ratio per price bucket. Numbers above bars = game count in each bucket.

§ 10

Platforms — Where Survival Lives

Where a game launches shapes who stays.

PC games dominate our dataset, but the survival gap between platform families is smaller than expected. Multi-platform releases show a modest vitality advantage — the larger the potential audience, the more likely someone keeps the candle lit.

Platform is a signal, not a sentence.

§ 13

The Slow Burn

Small launches, loyal communities.

These games never topped the Steam charts. They launched quietly, found their audience, and held on. Their player histories aren't spikes followed by collapse — they're slow, steady plateaus that run for years.

A devoted minority beats a fickle majority.

Hover any panel — the time marker syncs across all 6 charts.

§ 14

The Coverage Multiplier

Cultural memory compounds.

Games that stay alive receive 1.5× more YouTube coverage per year than games that fade. The coverage doesn't cause the survival — but it amplifies it. New players discover old games. Old players return. The loop closes.

Survival creates coverage. Coverage creates survival.

Distribution of YouTube videos/year. Green = high-vitality games (alive_ratio above median). Red = low-vitality.

§ 15

The Verdict

Survival isn't luck.

We combined alive_ratio, community engagement, and coverage into a single composite index — a mortality score for each game we tracked. These are the 25 that scored highest.

The pattern is consistent: endless design, community gravity, and cultural feedback. The games at the top all have at least two of these three.
